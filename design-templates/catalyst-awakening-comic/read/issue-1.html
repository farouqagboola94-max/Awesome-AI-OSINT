Arc I: The Catalyst · Issue 01
AWAKEN, O CITY
88 Pages · Free · Expanding toward 100
Panel 1 — 2:47 AM · Mushin, Lagos
Before Everything
Mushin never actually sleeps. It rests with one eye open. Tonight the open eye has seen something it was not meant to see.

Adebayo Adeyemi — Bayo to anyone who knows him, "that Mushin boy" to anyone who doesn't — is nineteen years old and running. Not from anything dramatic. From four area boys who think he stole a phone charger from a junction stall. He didn't. He was sitting near it. That was enough.

He has thirty-two naira in his pocket. His mother died six weeks ago. He has slept in four different places since the burial. None of them beds.

CAPTION —
Three weeks homeless. Before that: her apprentice mechanic on Paul Street. Before that: her son. In that order — she always said — is how a man should measure himself.

He ducks into an alley behind the old Odeon shell. Dead end. He turns. The four area boys slow, spreading out. They are not in a hurry. He has nowhere to go.

AREA BOY —
Oya, bring the charger. Nobody go touch you.
BAYO —
I no steal anything, my guy. I was just sitting down—

The first punch catches him across the jaw. He goes into the wall. His vision goes white at the edges. And then — the world changes.

It comes from above. Not metaphorically. From directly above the Balogun market, four hundred metres north. A tear. Like fabric. Like reality has a seam and something has found it.
Panel 2 — The Ọ̀run-Bleed
What the City Didn't Know to Fear

The tear in the sky above Balogun Nexus is thirty metres wide and getting larger. Through it: not darkness, not fire — light the wrong colour. A purple-gold that does not exist in any Lagos sunset. The street freezes. Cars stop. Generators cut out in sequence for three blocks in every direction.

From the tear, they fall. The Ajele — spirit-agents of an Orisha whose name no one in the crowd knows yet. They look human enough, until they move. Too fast. Too precise. Like something that learned how to pretend to have a body but hasn't quite committed to the lie.

Thunderstrike arrives first. Amara is good — she is genuinely, terrifyingly capable. Her talking drum fires thunderclaps that shatter concrete. She takes out seven Ajele in ninety seconds. Then the eighth catches her drum mid-beat and absorbs the sound. The silence that follows is louder than the thunder was.

Iron Wolf hits the ground from the overpass — Ikenna's iron skin clanging against the asphalt. He punches through three Ajele with satisfying finality. The fourteenth one lifts him off the ground with one hand and throws him through the air. He lands somewhere over the Third Mainland Bridge. He'll be fine. Eventually.

STREET CAPTION —
The crowd runs. Every direction. The danfo drivers abandon their vehicles. Even the most senior area boy in Balogun, a man who has never retreated from anything in thirty years of Lagos life, is already four streets away and accelerating.
Panel 3 — The Moment
He Doesn't Know Why. He Does It Anyway.

Bayo — who was supposed to be unconscious in an alley — has somehow ended up at the edge of the Balogun Nexus. He cannot explain it later. His feet moved. That's all he knows.

An Ajele turns toward him. Raises what it uses as a hand. Fires a bolt of compressed Aṣẹ — divine energy, raw and unprocessed, the kind that levels walls.

Bayo doesn't move. Not because he is brave. Because something in him is already reaching out before his brain has formed the thought. His right palm comes up, and the bolt hits it, and—

Silence. Then. The bolt comes back. Ten times the size. The Ajele catches it and ceases to exist as a concept. The shockwave clears the entire northern half of Balogun Market of every standing structure that wasn't load-bearing.

Bayo stares at his hand. It is not glowing. It is not special-looking. It is just a hand. A nineteen-year-old mechanic's apprentice's hand, calluses from Paul Street still in the lines of the palm.

BAYO —
...wetin happen?
THE ORACLE (from behind, very quietly) —
It's you. Of all people, of all the souls in this city... e don reach your turn.
PANEL 4 — Full-page spread · 2:58 AM · Balogun Nexus, above street level
The System Initializes
VISUAL: Bayo from above — tiny against the wreckage. Smoke rising. Three blocks of Balogun Market flattened in a perfect radius. In the centre: one nineteen-year-old with his hand still raised. The city is silent for the first time in living memory. No generators. No horns. Nothing.
Then: floating Yoruba script appears at the edge of his vision. Gold. Precise. Rotating slowly clockwise. He blinks. It stays. He closes his eyes. It brightens.
[ ASẸ SYSTEM — INITIALIZATION SEQUENCE ]
NEW VESSEL DETECTED · SCANNING...
IDENTITY: ADEBAYO ADEYEMI · AGE: 19 · ORIGIN: MUSHIN, LAGOS STATE
ORISHA AFFINITY SCAN: RUNNING...
[ WARNING: MULTIPLE SIMULTANEOUS AFFINITIES DETECTED — CLASSIFICATION ANOMALY ]
ẸṢÙ RESONANCE: 91% · ṢÀNGÓ RESONANCE: 64% · ÒGÚN RESONANCE: 83%
OYÁ RESONANCE: 45% · ỌṢUN RESONANCE: 57%
[ ANOMALY: No vessel in recorded history has held simultaneous multi-Orisha affinity above 40% ]
GENERATING MANIFESTATION IDENTITY DESCRIPTOR (MID)...
MID ASSIGNED: ADY·19·Λ:ESU-A/OGU-A/SAN-A·TIER:UNCLASSIFIED·CLASS:CATALYST·FLAG:ÌMỌ̀LẸ̀-ÀÁRỌ̀
MID-CLASS: ÌMỌ̀LẸ̀-ÀÁRỌ̀ [MORNING LIGHT] — No prior instance in 4,000-year Ifá records.
WELCOME, VESSEL. THE SYSTEM HAS BEEN WAITING.
BAYO (internal) —
This is not real. This is what happens when you get punched and hit a wall. This is a concussion. This is—
[ ASẸ SYSTEM — NOTE ]
You are not concussed. Aṣẹ capacity currently at 3% of maximum. Recommend immediate caution. Every Orisha in the pantheon is looking at this location right now.
All of them. Simultaneously.
PANEL 5 — Three-panel tier · Chief Adesanya's compound, Idumota · 3:00 AM exact
The Oracle Witnesses
VISUAL PANEL 1: Chief Adesanya Adewale — 144 years old, white-robed, sitting in complete darkness — suddenly goes rigid. His staff, which has not glowed in eleven years, ignites in five colours simultaneously. The wax candles around him light without flame touching them.
VISUAL PANEL 2: Close on his face. His eyes — clouded from a century of Aṣẹ-sight strain — are suddenly clear. He is seeing something he has not seen since 1987. He is seeing a MID pattern he has been drawing in sand for thirty-seven years, waiting.
VISUAL PANEL 3: The sand diagrams on his compound floor. Hundreds of them, overlapping. In the centre, the pattern he has been refining for decades. And in the smoke rising from the staff, the same pattern — exact, perfect, glowing gold. He was right. He has been right the whole time.
THE ORACLE (alone, to himself) —
...Ẹ̀bọ mi ti gbà. My sacrifice has been accepted. After thirty-seven years.
THE ORACLE —
He is in Balogun. He is confused. He is alone. And he has no idea — no idea at all — what he just started.
He rises. For a man who has been alive since 1882, he moves with remarkable urgency. He has somewhere to be. He has been going there in his mind for thirty-seven years. Now his feet can finally follow.
PANEL 6 — Double-page spread · Geneva, Switzerland · 3:04 AM (Lagos time) / 4:04 AM (local)
Geneva, That Same Minute
VISUAL: The Pale Council's primary monitoring floor — three floors below street level, white walls, blue-white screens, seventeen analysts in identical black uniforms. The floor is silent except for server cooling systems. Then: every single screen redlines simultaneously.
A Yoruba energy signature has just registered that doesn't fit any known classification. The system cycles through every existing MID taxonomy — vessels, conduits, amplifiers, collectors, seers — and returns the same result each time: NO MATCH.
ANALYST #7 —
Sir. Lagos. Balogun Nexus. Signature type is — the system says it's not classifiable. It can't categorize it.
DIRECTOR VALE (quiet, very still) —
Cross-reference with the Ìmọ̀lẹ̀ Protocol files.
ANALYST #7 —
The Ìmọ̀lẹ̀ files are theoretical, sir. They describe a category we've never actually confirmed exists—
DIRECTOR VALE —
Cross-reference anyway.
VISUAL: The cross-reference result appears on the main screen. 99.7% match. Director Vale does not move for fourteen seconds. Then, precisely:
DIRECTOR VALE —
Wake the Architect. Tell her Lagos has produced what Lagos was always going to produce. And tell her we needed to be there six months ago.
PANEL 7 — Nine-panel grid · Internal monologue sequence · Balogun Nexus, 3:22 AM
Thirty-Two Naira
VISUAL STRUCTURE: Nine equal panels, black gutters, each panel a different aspect of the same moment — Bayo standing alone in the rubble. Panel 1: his face. Panel 2: his hands. Panel 3: the floating Aṣẹ script he alone can see. Panel 4: the ruined market behind him. Panel 5: the tear in the sky, still closing. Panel 6: his feet — shoes with a split sole, duct-taped together. Panel 7: the thirty-two naira in his open palm. Panel 8: the city of Lagos, from high above, one small golden glow in Balogun. Panel 9: darkness.
BAYO (internal, caption boxes across all nine panels) —
My mother died six weeks ago. I have slept in four different places since the burial. None of them beds. I have thirty-two naira. I was running from area boys when this happened. I don't know what this is. I don't know what I am. I don't know if the thing I just did killed anyone or saved anyone. I don't know if I'm still me. I know I'm standing in Lagos at three in the morning. I know I'm cold. I know I'm hungry. I know whatever just came out of my hand — it came from somewhere I don't have a name for. And the city is still here. That has to mean something. That has to mean—
The Aṣẹ System, reading his internal state, does something unprecedented. It does not issue a quest. It does not display a stat. It generates a single line of Yoruba script, softly, at the lower edge of his vision, and then deletes it thirty seconds later as if it was never there. He reads it before it goes. He doesn't mention it to anyone for three arcs. The line reads:
ASẸ SYSTEM [UNREPEATED, UNLOGGED] —
"Ìyá rẹ ní kí n tọ́ ọ sọ̀nà." Your mother asked me to look after you.
PANEL 8 — Wide establishing shot + six rapid cuts · Balogun street-level, 3:31 AM
The Four, First Night
VISUAL: Wide shot — a danfo with the front end crumpled against a market stall. Ikenna emerges from behind it, iron plates re-knitting across his forearms. He is covered in structural dust and is annoyed. Amara descends from the overpass above — her talking drum slung across her back, one drumhead cracked. She is assessing the scene with professional calm. Zara materialises from a shadow sixteen metres to the left, silent, already watching all of them.
VISUAL: Four people in a ruined Lagos market at 3:31 AM. This is not a team. A team is built. This is four strangers who have just survived the same thing from different angles, standing in the dark, looking at each other with extreme caution.
IKENNA (to Bayo) —
You're the one who reflected the Ajele strike. You deflected something at the Class-6 threshold with your bare hand. How?
BAYO —
I don't know what Class-6 means.
IKENNA —
It means impossible.
BAYO —
Okay. I don't know how I did impossible, then.
AMARA (not looking at any of them, examining her drum) —
The Oracle will know. If the Oracle doesn't know, then this is something genuinely new. And if it's genuinely new—
ZARA (from the shadows, first words of the series) —
Then every enemy we have will be here within six weeks. Which means we have exactly six weeks to figure out what it is before they do.
VISUAL FINAL PANEL: The four of them in frame together for the first time. No noble pose. No dramatic stance. Bayo with split-soled shoes and thirty-two naira. Amara with a cracked drum. Ikenna still shedding iron dust. Zara half in shadow by choice. The Oracle's silhouette visible approaching from the north. This is what the beginning looks like.
PANEL 9 — The walk to Idumota · 4:10 AM, first light not yet up
Four Strangers, One Direction
VISUAL: Wide tracking shot along Nnamdi Azikiwe Street, the four of them walking in a loose, uneven line behind the Oracle's silhouette. Nobody has assigned an order to the walk. It has assigned itself — Ikenna at the front because he is used to leading with his shoulders, Zara at the back because she has never once in her life walked in front of anyone she doesn't trust, Amara and Bayo in the middle because neither of them has decided yet whether they belong there.

Nobody talks for the first six minutes. Lagos is doing the thing it does in the hour before it wakes — bread sellers setting up stalls that won't open for two more hours, a lone okada idling under a dead streetlight, the smell of frying akara starting to compete with the smell of the night's rain drying off the tarmac. None of it looks like a city that nearly lost three blocks of its oldest market forty minutes ago. Lagos has survived worse and simply declined to remember it.

AMARA (finally, to break it) —
So. Nobody's going to ask the obvious question?
IKENNA —
Which one. There's four obvious questions.
AMARA —
Why does an old man we've never met get to just say "follow me" and all three of us — four of us — just... follow?

Bayo, who has been quiet since Balogun, since the market, since the version of himself that existed before 2:47 AM stopped being available to him, surprises himself by answering first.

BAYO —
Because he's the only person tonight who looked at me like I wasn't a problem to be solved.

Nobody argues with that. Zara, from the back, makes a small sound that might be agreement or might just be Zara acknowledging that a true thing was said out loud, which she does rarely and reluctantly.

ZARA —
He's also the only person in this city who's been preparing for tonight since before any of us were born. That buys some trust. Not all of it.
VISUAL: Ikenna glances back at Bayo — a long, assessing look, mechanic to mechanic's apprentice, the closest thing to solidarity he's willing to show a stranger yet. He says nothing. He doesn't need to. Bayo catches the look and, for the first time since the alley, feels something other than fear or confusion. Something almost like being seen.
PANEL 10 — Chief Adesanya's compound, Idumota · sunrise
What "Catalyst" Actually Means
VISUAL: The compound as the lore remembers it — a low, wide house with a corrugated roof gone soft-orange with rust, a courtyard floor covered in overlapping sand diagrams drawn and redrawn for thirty-seven years, the smell the compound is known for: ìtò, palm oil, paper gone soft with age. Dawn light through the compound's east wall makes every diagram on the floor briefly gold before the sun clears the wall and it all goes back to ordinary sand.

The Oracle does not offer chairs immediately. He walks to the centre of the largest diagram, crouches with the care of a man who has done this exact motion ten thousand times, and studies it as if checking his own arithmetic.

THE ORACLE —
Sit. All of you. What I am about to say, I have never said aloud to another living person. Not because it was secret. Because until forty minutes ago, I could not have proven it was true.

He gestures at the diagram beneath him — spirals within spirals, five distinct colour-stains worked into the sand where he has, over decades, mixed in crushed camwood, indigo, and things none of the four recognise.

THE ORACLE —
Every vessel the Orishas have ever chosen — every one, across four thousand years of record — has belonged to one. Ògún's smiths. Ṣàngó's warriors. Ọya's stormcallers. Ọ̀ṣun's healers. Ẹṣù's tricksters, translators, thieves, and diplomats — the same Orisha wearing different coats depending on what the crossroads demanded that century. One Orisha. One vessel. That has been the entire architecture of Aṣẹ since before Lagos had a name.
IKENNA —
And Bayo isn't that.
THE ORACLE —
Bayo registered above forty percent resonance with four Orishas simultaneously, in the same second, with no training and no warning. That has never happened. I am one hundred and forty-four years old. I have been Aṣẹ-sighted since I was nine. I have never seen a reading like this, and I have never heard of one — and I have spent sixty years reading everything there is to read.

He looks up, directly at Bayo, and for the first time his ancient calm cracks — not into fear, but into something that looks uncomfortably like awe.

THE ORACLE —
A vessel is a cup. It holds what it is given and takes the shape of the Orisha who fills it. You are not a cup, Adebayo Adeyemi. Your system named you correctly. You are a Catalyst. You do not hold power. You change what touches you — and it changes you back, all at once, all directions. Every Orisha who has ever wanted this city's Aṣẹ for themselves is about to discover that the rules they've relied on for four thousand years no longer fully apply to one nineteen-year-old boy from Mushin.
BAYO —
So what happens to a cup that isn't a cup?
THE ORACLE (quietly) —
Some try to break it, to see what spills. Some try to claim it before anyone else can. And some — the patient ones, the dangerous ones — will simply try to decide, on your behalf, what shape you should have been all along.
He lets that sit. Outside the compound wall, Lagos has fully woken — danfo horns, a radio somewhere playing fuji at a volume that assumes everyone wants to hear it, the ordinary roar of nine million people starting an ordinary Tuesday that is, for four of them, no longer ordinary at all.
PANEL 11 — A back room of the compound · mid-morning
Thirty-Two Naira, Six Weeks Ago
VISUAL: A small room off the courtyard — a single window, a mat, the kind of spare cleanliness that suggests it hasn't been used in years but was kept ready anyway. Bayo sits on the edge of the mat, turning the thirty-two naira over in his palm. Not because it's worth anything. Because it's the last object his old life gave him before it ended.

He doesn't hear Amara approach. She has, apparently, decided this is her job now — nobody assigned it to her either, the same way nobody assigned Ikenna the front of the line.

AMARA —
You've been staring at that coin for eleven minutes. I counted.
BAYO —
My mother used to say a man should always know exactly what's in his pocket. Not because of the money. Because if you know exactly what you have, nobody can lie to you about what you're missing.

He doesn't cry. He has been very deliberately not crying for six weeks, and tonight — this morning, technically, though it doesn't feel like morning yet in any way that matters — does not feel like the moment to stop being deliberate about it.

FLASHBACK PANEL, desaturated to near-monochrome except for one warm lamp: A hospital corridor in Mushin General, six weeks prior. Bayo, younger-looking somehow despite it being weeks not years, sitting outside a ward door. A nurse who has clearly delivered this exact sentence to clearly too many people this month, saying it as gently as repetition allows.
NURSE (flashback) —
She's asking for you. She doesn't have long, Bayo. Go in.
FLASHBACK PANEL: His mother, thin against hospital linen, one hand finding his without needing to look for it — the hand-finding of a woman who has held that hand since it was small enough to close entirely around one of her fingers.
MOTHER (flashback, weak but clear) —
You are going to feel like nothing is holding you up soon. I need you to remember something isn't the same as nothing. Something is always holding you up, Bayo. You just won't always be able to name it yet.

Back in the present, Amara sits on the floor across from him — not next to him, across, close enough to matter and far enough to respect that he hasn't invited closer.

AMARA —
For what it's worth — and I know it's not worth much from someone you met four hours ago — you didn't do anything wrong tonight. You didn't ask for any of this. You just happened to be standing in the one spot in Lagos where the whole city's luck decided to change hands.
BAYO —
The Oracle called it a gift. Ikenna called it impossible. You're calling it luck. I don't know which one of you is right.
AMARA —
Maybe none of us are, yet. Maybe that's the whole point of the next hundred pages, Bayo — finding out together, before somebody with a lot more resources than the Oracle decides to answer the question for you.
PANEL 12 — Split scene: Lagos rooftop / Pale Council field team · same morning
Six Months Late
VISUAL: A rooftop overlooking the Balogun wreckage from four hundred metres, already visible even in daylight — a scorched, flattened crescent where three blocks of stalls used to stand, cordoned off by confused-looking LASTMA officials with no framework for what actually happened. A woman in a grey field jacket watches through compact binoculars. She has been in Lagos eleven hours. She was supposed to have six months.
FIELD OPERATIVE (into a wrist-comm, clipped) —
Confirmed visual on the blast radius. Whatever hit this market did it with a single discharge. No secondary detonations, no accelerant, no explanation the local authorities are willing to write down. Requesting full asset authorisation for Lagos.
DIRECTOR VALE (through comm, from Geneva) —
Authorisation granted before you asked. The Architect lands in nine hours. Until then, observe only. Do not engage the subject, do not engage anyone near the subject, and do not — under any circumstance — let Lagos know we were ever anywhere else in the world when this started.

The operative lowers the binoculars. Down at street level, small against the ruined market, four figures and an old man in white are disappearing east, toward Idumota, unhurried, like people who have nowhere urgent to be — which is, the operative reflects, either supreme confidence or a total failure to grasp what is about to be pointed at them.

FIELD OPERATIVE —
Copy. Observation only. For the record, sir — the file said we had until this boy turned twenty to prepare. He's nineteen.
DIRECTOR VALE —
The file was wrong. The file has been wrong about Lagos for sixty years. That is precisely why I am the one still awake at four in the morning, and why you should be very glad you're only observing.
VISUAL, final: the compound gate at Idumota closing behind the five of them. From the rooftop, four hundred metres and one very large information gap away, it looks like nothing. It is, in fact, the single most consequential door in Lagos closing for the night — or what's left of it.
PANEL 13 — Compound courtyard, a step back from the group · mid-morning
Zara Osei, First Full Look
VISUAL: Zara has not sat down since arriving. She stands at the courtyard's one shaded corner, exactly where she can see both compound doors and neither door can see her clearly first. This is not paranoia. This is a discipline, built over years, the way other people build a jump shot or a chord progression.

She is twenty-six. Ògún chose her at fourteen, in a Kumasi market three countries from here — not the smith's Ògún that Ikenna carries, but the older aspect, the one who cuts roads through places that had no road, who is patron of anyone who has ever had to clear their own path out of somewhere they weren't meant to leave. She has spent the twelve years since learning exactly one lesson over and over: the crossroads favours whoever notices the exit first. She does not fight the way Ikenna fights or the way Amara fights. She simply arranges to never be where the fight expects her.

IKENNA —
You said "every enemy we have" back at the market. Not "every enemy Bayo has." You already knew there was a "we" before there was a team.
ZARA —
I've been tracking Pale Council movement into West Africa for nine months. Three shell logistics companies, two "agricultural research" grants that have never once mentioned agriculture, and a flight manifest out of Geneva that lands in Lagos in eight hours with a passenger list that doesn't exist. I didn't know it would be a boy from Mushin. I knew it would be someone.
AMARA —
And you're telling us this now instead of nine months ago because—
ZARA (flat, not unkind) —
Because nine months ago it was theory, and I don't spend other people's fear on theories. Now it's a flight manifest. Now it's worth spending.
VISUAL: A beat where nobody quite trusts her and everybody quietly recalculates how useful that distrust would be to argue with. Bayo watches this exchange from across the courtyard and understands, for the first time, that the team he has just joined was already at war before he ever raised his hand in Balogun.
PANEL 14 — Six-panel grid · Courtyard, one hour later
The First Test
VISUAL GRID, six equal panels: the Oracle has drawn a fresh circle in clean sand, unmarked by thirty-seven years of diagrams. Ikenna volunteers first, out of the particular impatience of a man who fixes things for a living and wants to know, mechanically, what he's dealing with.
IKENNA —
Hit my arm. Not hard. Half of what you did at the market.

Panel one: Bayo's palm meets Ikenna's forearm. Panel two: nothing visible happens — no flash, no shockwave. Panel three: Ikenna's iron plating, which has not fully "woken" — Ògún's word, relayed through the Oracle — since he manifested three years ago, suddenly runs bright orange along every seam, like a blade freshly quenched. Panel four: Ikenna staring at his own arm like he's never met it. Panel five, his voice quieter than anyone has heard it all day:

IKENNA —
It's never done that. Three years, and it's never once done that.
THE ORACLE —
A Catalyst does not add power. He completes what is already reaching for its own ceiling. Ikenna's iron has been asleep at ninety percent for three years because nothing has ever asked it to finish waking. Bayo just asked.

Panel six: Amara steps up next, cautiously, her cracked drum held slightly away from her body like it might bite. It does not bite. Instead, at the barest brush of Bayo's fingers against the drumhead, the crack seals itself — not repaired, exactly, but resolved, the way a held breath resolves when it's finally let out.

AMARA (staring at the drum) —
Okay. Okay, that's — that's genuinely terrifying and I would like to do it again immediately.
PANEL 15 — The same courtyard, four minutes later
The Thing Nobody Tested For
VISUAL: Riding the small high of two successful, harmless demonstrations, Zara — against her own better instinct, which she will spend the rest of the day annoyed about — allows Bayo to touch the flat of her palm, curious what a Catalyst does to someone whose entire gift is subtraction, not addition.

The Oracle sees it half a second before it happens and is already shouting a warning that arrives too late by exactly half a second.

THE ORACLE (too late) —
Not her — Zara's gift is absence, he cannot complete an absence, he can only—
VISUAL, full-width panel: The moment Bayo's fingers touch her palm, Zara does not glow. She flickers — present, then faintly transparent, then present again, like a signal losing and regaining a station, three times in under two seconds. For those two seconds she is standing in the courtyard and simultaneously, faintly, standing in at least two other places she has stood before. The Oracle's diagrams in the sand around them all shift half an inch, as if something briefly rearranged the ground itself to make room for her.
ZARA (very still, very controlled, visibly shaken beneath it) —
Don't. Do that again. Ever.
BAYO —
I didn't do anything, I just—
ZARA —
I know exactly what you just did. You asked a question my gift was built specifically never to answer. Don't ask it again until I say you can.

She walks to the far side of the courtyard and does not return for eleven minutes. When she does, she says nothing about it, and nobody — not even Amara, who says something about everything — asks.

PANEL 16 — Paul Street Workshop, Mushin · early afternoon
The Job He's Supposed to Still Have
VISUAL: Bayo alone at the workshop where he apprenticed for three years, the smell of engine oil and iron dust unchanged since his mother walked him here at sixteen. His boss, Baba Fọlá, sixty-one years old, oil permanently under his fingernails, does not look up from the gearbox he's rebuilding.
BABA FỌLÁ —
Three days you no come work. No message, nothing. I almost give your bench to Rasheed's boy.
BAYO —
Baba, something happen. Big something. I no fit explain am well well, but—
BABA FỌLÁ (finally looking up, and seeing something in Bayo's face he recognises from thirty years of Lagos mornings) —
Your eyes don change since Monday. Whatever this "big something" be, e never finish with you.

Bayo doesn't answer immediately. He looks at the bench that has been his for three years — the specific dent in the vice, the specific crack in the stool leg he's meant to fix for six months and never has. Thirty-two naira and a dead mother used to be the entire shape of his life. Now there is an Oracle, a system that speaks Yoruba script into his vision, three strangers who are somehow already closer to him than anyone but his mother ever was, and a flight from Geneva that lands in — he checks the time on the workshop's cracked wall clock — six hours.

BAYO —
Baba, I need to keep my bench. Not because I need the job. Because I need one thing in my life that's still just mine. Just... normal. Please.
BABA FỌLÁ (returning to the gearbox, gruffly, which is his version of tenderness) —
Bench still dey there. Fix the stool leg before you carry your "big something" and disappear again.
VISUAL, final: Bayo alone at his bench, actually fixing the stool leg, for exactly six minutes, before the ASẸ System flares gently at the edge of his vision — not urgent, not alarmed, just present, the way it will now always be present. He finishes the repair anyway. Some things, he decides, still deserve to be finished properly.
PANEL 17 — Murtala Muhammed International Airport, Private Charter Apron · 7:58 PM
Not the Architect. Not Yet.
VISUAL: A grey unmarked charter taxis to a stop away from the main terminal, on the apron Lagos customs reserves for the kind of visitor who arrives with a diplomatic note instead of a passport photo. Six operatives disembark in civilian clothes that are, to a trained eye, aggressively not civilian — Zara, watching through a stolen ground-crew vest and a headset, clocks all six before the stairs finish unfolding.

Not the Architect. The Oracle was right about that much — she does not fly commercial-adjacent charters into cities she hasn't finished assessing. This is a forward team. Assessment, containment planning, and — Zara notes, with the specific coldness of someone doing math she doesn't like — one of the six is carrying a case built to the exact dimensions of a live sample transport unit.

TEAM LEAD (to the airport liaison, in flawless but oddly accentless English) —
Agricultural assessment team. Our paperwork should already be with your ministry contact.
VISUAL: The Nigerian customs officer checking the manifest does not know, and will never know, that the "agricultural assessment team" designation is the same cover the Pale Council has used in eleven other countries in the last four years, each time immediately preceding what their internal documents call, without any apparent irony, a "resonance acquisition event."
ZARA (subvocal, into a compound-linked earpiece) —
Six on the ground. One carrying acquisition hardware. They're not here to observe anymore. Whatever changed, changed in the last six hours.
THE ORACLE (through the earpiece, unhurried, ancient) —
What changed is that Geneva finally believes its own instruments. Return to the compound. Do not engage six operatives alone, however capable you feel tonight.
PANEL 18 — Third Mainland Bridge, underside access road · 9:40 PM
First Contact
VISUAL: Zara's return route takes her under the bridge's service span — her preferred route precisely because it has no working streetlights and four exits. Tonight it has a seventh person in it who shouldn't be there: one of the six from the apron, standing very still in a spot that a normal person would not think to check.
PALE COUNCIL OPERATIVE (not turning around) —
You've been good, these nine months. Careful. We only found your pattern because you got curious about our shell logistics filings four times more than any other analyst would. Curiosity is a tell, Miss Osei.

Zara does not confirm her name. She also does not run, which the operative notices, which is exactly the reaction she wanted him to notice.

ZARA —
You knew my name before you landed. So this isn't a chance meeting under a bridge. What do you actually want?
OPERATIVE —
A message, delivered exactly once, to whoever the Oracle is protecting. The Council does not require Lagos's cooperation. We are simply, at this stage, still willing to ask for it. That courtesy has a very specific expiration date, and it is shorter than anyone here would like.
VISUAL: He sets a plain black card on a concrete pillar — no markings, faintly warm to the touch, humming at a frequency only Aṣẹ-sighted eyes can register — and walks, unhurried, into the dark the way he arrived: without appearing to have used any of the exits Zara was watching.
ZARA (alone, to the empty underside of the bridge) —
...he walked through a wall. He physically walked through a support pillar. Oracle, we need to talk about what "agricultural assessment team" actually means.
PANEL 19 — Full-issue finale · Oracle's compound courtyard · 11:15 PM
The Card
VISUAL: The black card sits in the centre of the same sand diagram where, less than a day ago, an Aṣẹ system first spoke Bayo's name. The Oracle has not touched it in four minutes. Everyone else has learned, in one very long day, to wait when he goes quiet like this.
THE ORACLE (finally) —
This is not Pale Council technology. This is Pale Council technology built from something they should not have had access to. This card is warm because it is drawing, very slowly, on someone's Aṣẹ signature to stay active. Someone they are keeping. Or someone they have already taken.
IKENNA —
So Day One, and we already know they've done this before. Somewhere. To someone.
AMARA —
Then Day One isn't when this started for us. It's just the day we found out it had already been happening.

Bayo picks up the card. Nobody stops him — the Oracle, watching closely, seems almost to be waiting for exactly this. The instant it touches his palm, the card's faint hum stops being faint. Beneath the surface, for less than a second, a name resolves in old, worn Yoruba script — a name none of the five of them recognise, gone before anyone but Bayo can read it in full.

BAYO (quiet, shaken) —
There's somebody in this. Not data. A person. I felt them for half a second, and then the card shut them back down like closing a door on someone still screaming.
VISUAL, double-page finale spread: The five of them around the sand diagram at midnight, the compound's one working bulb throwing long shadows, the black card now dark and cold in Bayo's palm. Outside the walls, Lagos continues exactly as it always does — danfo horns, generators, nine million people who have no idea that the city's oldest secret war just changed its terms for the first time in decades.
THE ORACLE —
Thirty-seven years I prepared for the day a Catalyst arrived. Not one hour of it prepared me for what he'd feel when he touched their evidence. We do not have weeks to understand what you are, Adebayo. We may not have days. Whoever is trapped in that card is the first proof this war has always had casualties we never counted. Tonight, that changes.
End of Issue #01 · Continue in Issue #02 — ṢÀNGÓ'S DAUGHTER
A stranger's name, half-read in dying script, now lives somewhere in Bayo's memory that wasn't there yesterday. Six Pale Council operatives are already in Lagos. And four hundred kilometres east, in a tower built on reclaimed land, something in a sub-basement laboratory has just started, for the first time in months, to hope.
